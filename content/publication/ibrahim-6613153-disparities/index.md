abstract: |
  The COVID-19 pandemic exposed socioeconomic vulnerabilities, disproportionately affecting marginalized groups such as immigrants in the United States. Using data from, the   Socioeconomic Impacts of COVID-19 Survey (SEICS), a national longitudinal, survey from the Social Policy Institute, this study examined the disparate economic, hardships impact experienced by immigrant households. The pooled sample for this, analysis included respondents with at least two waves of data for a total of 4,910, unique participants and 15,301 participant-wave observations. Bivariate analysis and, logistic regression models with random effects were used to identify statistically, significant disparities in financial hardships. Our findings indicate that immigrant, households experienced a complex array of hardships, including difficulties in housing, payment, forgone medical care, and a reliance on high-cost alternative financial, services (AFS) such as auto title and payday loans. Even after controlling for, sociodemographic factors like income and employment status, immigrant households, had significantly higher odds of experiencing financial distress. They were over twice, as likely to report difficulty with housing payments and nearly two times more likely to, forgo medical care due to cost compared to their non-immigrant counterparts., Immigrant households were also found to be 2.5 times more likely to use auto title, loans and nearly twice as likely to use payday loans. Our findings suggest that these, disparities are not solely due to individual/household characteristics, but are a result of, structural and institutional inequities. The exclusion of immigrants from federal relief, programs like the CARES Act and the “chilling effect” of the “public charge” rule, pushed these vulnerable communities toward exploitative financial services. These, findings have policy and practice implications for children and youth. The, findings highlight the need for inclusive policy interventions that protect all residents., Study implications with specific focus on children and youth, and limitations are discussed.
